Seasonal Agriculture Performance Analysis¶

VOIS AICTE Batch1 2026-2027 — Major Project

Dataset: seasonal_agriculture_performance_dataset.csv (4,000 farm records)

Objective: Analyze how agricultural performance — yield, resource usage, and profitability — changes across the three Indian cropping seasons (Kharif, Rabi and Zaid), and identify patterns that can support better seasonal planning.

This notebook was built and run in Google Colab. The workflow follows: data loading → cleaning → exploration → seasonal comparison → correlation analysis → insights → recommendations.

Three columns have missing values — Rainfall_mm, Soil_Moisture_pct and Yield_Tonnes_Ha. All three are small (1-1.2% of rows), so dropping them isn't necessary. Instead of filling with a single global median (which would blur seasonal differences), missing rainfall/soil moisture values are filled with the season median, and missing yield values are filled with the crop + season median since yield varies a lot by crop.

In [7]:
df['Rainfall_mm'] = df.groupby('Season')['Rainfall_mm'].transform(lambda x: x.fillna(x.median()))
df['Soil_Moisture_pct'] = df.groupby('Season')['Soil_Moisture_pct'].transform(lambda x: x.fillna(x.median()))
df['Yield_Tonnes_Ha'] = df.groupby(['Crop', 'Season'])['Yield_Tonnes_Ha'].transform(lambda x: x.fillna(x.median()))

This is probably the most important finding in the whole analysis. Even though average cost per farm is fairly similar across seasons (roughly ₹5.1-5.4 lakh), the outcome is very different:

  • Kharif farms average a profit of ₹1.79 lakh, and only 42% run at a loss.
  • Rabi farms average just ₹88K profit, with just over half (51%) running at a loss.
  • Zaid farms average an outright loss of ₹25K, and nearly two-thirds (64.5%) of Zaid farms lose money.

So the season with the least rainfall and highest heat isn't just lower-yielding — it's financially the riskiest one to farm in, under this dataset.

7. What's Actually Driving Yield and Profit? — Correlation Check¶

A few things stand out here that weren't obvious just from the seasonal averages:

  • Water efficiency correlates very strongly with yield (r = 0.92) — basically, farms that convert water into crop tonnage efficiently are the ones getting good yields, more so than any single environmental factor.
  • Fertilizer use has almost zero correlation with yield (r ≈ 0.00). This was a bit surprising — more fertilizer isn't translating into more yield in this dataset, which could mean farms are already at a saturation point, or fertilizer isn't the limiting factor here.
  • Market price is negatively correlated with yield (r = -0.38). This makes sense once you think about it: high-yield crops like Sugarcane sell at a lower price per tonne than low-yield, high-value crops like Chilli — so raw yield alone doesn't tell you about profitability.
  • Rainfall correlates with disease/pest risk (r = 0.62) — wetter conditions bring more pest and disease pressure, which is a real agronomic pattern (and probably explains why Kharif has the highest risk score despite also having the highest yield, see below).

Interestingly, the irrigation method mix barely changes across seasons — Flood stays the most common method (~33%) in all three, followed by Rainfed, Drip, and Sprinkler in roughly the same proportions. So farmers don't appear to be switching irrigation strategy by season in this data, which itself is a bit of a finding (it's not a variable that explains the seasonal yield/profit gap).

Disease/pest risk, on the other hand, does shift clearly by season — Kharif is highest at 54.5%, consistent with the rainfall-risk correlation seen earlier, while Rabi and Zaid sit lower at ~40% and ~38%.

10. Key Insights¶

  1. Kharif is the strongest season overall — highest average yield (5.63 t/ha), highest average profit (₹1.79 lakh/farm), and the lowest share of loss-making farms (42%).
  2. Zaid is the weakest and riskiest season — lowest yield (4.64 t/ha), an average loss of ₹25K/farm, and 64.5% of farms losing money. High heat and low rainfall/soil moisture during Zaid appear to be the main drag.
  3. Water efficiency, not fertilizer, is the strongest predictor of yield (r = 0.92 vs r ≈ 0.00 for fertilizer). Fertilizer input doesn't seem to be the bottleneck here.
  4. Yield and market price move in opposite directions (r = -0.38) — high-yield bulk crops (Sugarcane) fetch lower per-tonne prices than low-yield, higher-value crops (Chilli, Cotton), so yield alone is a poor proxy for profitability.
  5. Rainfall drives up disease/pest risk (r = 0.62), which is likely why Kharif — despite being the best-performing season — also carries the highest average pest/disease risk score (54.5%).
  6. Irrigation method choice does not vary meaningfully by season — Flood remains the dominant method (~33% share) across all three seasons, so it isn't a factor explaining the seasonal performance gap.
  7. The State/District fields in this dataset are not geographically consistent (each district appears across all states in similar proportions), so any regional patterns should be treated as categorical, not real geography.

11. Conclusions & Recommendations¶

Season has a clear, measurable effect on agricultural outcomes in this dataset — not just on yield, but more importantly on profitability and risk. Zaid, in particular, stands out as a season where a majority of farms are currently losing money, which is worth flagging for planning purposes.

Recommendations:

  • Farmers cultivating in Zaid season may benefit from water-efficient crop choices or drip/sprinkler irrigation, since water efficiency is the strongest yield driver identified here, and Zaid already has the lowest rainfall and soil moisture.
  • Given that fertilizer showed almost no relationship with yield, input costs going into fertilizer during low-yield seasons like Zaid could be re-evaluated rather than increased.
  • Because Kharif combines the highest yield with the highest disease/pest risk, seasonal pest-management advisories may be more valuable in Kharif than the other two seasons.
  • Since raw yield doesn't guarantee profit (given the negative yield-price correlation), season-wise crop selection should weigh expected market price alongside expected yield, not yield alone.
  • Future versions of this dataset would benefit from consistent, real State-District geography, so genuine regional seasonal patterns (e.g. differences by agro-climatic zone) could also be studied.
